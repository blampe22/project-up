The Story · November 22, 2026

The Day Preconstruction Got Out of Hand

It started with a nursery mural for Auggie and Theo. Divide the artwork into forty panels, let the family paint them, and put the pieces together into one big adventure. It was a lovely, simple idea right up until Ben decided to run it like a construction job.

In the running joke, Katie played the demanding Owner. In real life she was supportive from the start. Ben volunteered for the paperwork all on his own. Soon a family painting project had a Project Manual, a Gantt schedule, a stack of addenda, and a website.

Then came the first meaningful inspection of the website. Eleven addenda had already gone out. Addendum No. 012 documented the Architect’s premature confidence and prompted a round of quality-control corrections. Somewhere in that review, the team realized a simple email could do the job we’d been designing an entire registration database to handle.

Ben took to delegation with enthusiasm. Theostus and Codex earned formal recognition as project consultants, and somewhere between handing off assignments and checking what came back, Ben realized he’d learned how to build a website.

When he finally looked up fabrication on his own Gantt schedule, the start date was December 5. As of November 22, the Owner hadn’t even seen the website. Preconstruction, on the other hand, had thoroughly enjoyed itself.

Project UP is where imagination meets probability, and even the fun has a contingency allowance. Under all the paperwork was the reason the family started: making something together as a family that Auggie and Theo can enjoy for years.

From the Project Trailer

Owner Review No. 001 — The Executive Dashboard Directive

After an extensive preconstruction effort involving twelve addenda, a comprehensive Project Manual, a master schedule, and an interactive website, Project UP was finally presented to its Owner.

Katie reviewed the proposed mural.

Her first comment was immediate.

Katie: “40 panels?!”

Ben, who had somehow made it this far without fully appreciating the implications of manufacturing forty individual pieces, offered a reassuring response.

Ben: “Uhh... yeah, I didn’t really think about it.”

This exchange marked the project's first formal constructability review.

Following additional evaluation, the Owner identified opportunities to improve readability and project progress visibility.

The project management team interpreted these observations as a formal directive requiring an Executive Reporting Dashboard.

This was particularly unfortunate timing for Ben.

During his actual working hours, he had been developing executive-level Power BI dashboards to track training completion, participation, and organizational performance.

Apparently, those skills were now required at home.

The nursery mural would need a similar level of executive oversight.

Forty panels. Individual fabrication statuses. Painting assignments. Completion percentages. Progress reporting.

The Owner had spoken.

Or, more accurately, the Owner had questioned the panel quantity and requested better visibility.

The AI project management department handled the scope expansion from there.
